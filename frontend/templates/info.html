The Estate

Coolattin and the Fitzwilliam Family

The Coolattin Estate was the Irish holding of the Earls Fitzwilliam, one of the wealthiest aristocratic families in Britain. The 5th Earl — Charles William Wentworth-Fitzwilliam — managed the estate through resident agents, receiving rents that formed a substantial part of the family's income.

Before the Famine the estate had a reputation for relatively benign management by the standards of the era. Tenants were not routinely rack-rented, and the agents' correspondence in the Fitzwilliam Papers suggests a degree of concern for tenant welfare.

That reputation came under severe strain when the potato blight arrived in 1845. As rent arrears mounted and the relief burden grew, the estate turned to systematic assisted emigration as the solution — clearing indebted tenants in exchange for passage to North America.

"The tenantry of Coolattin are, I believe, in a better condition than those of most other estates in Ireland." — Agent's report, c. 1842. Fitzwilliam Papers, NLI.

Whether this earlier assessment made the clearances more or less just is a question historians continue to debate. 📚 Sources

Society

The Estate Social Hierarchy

Irish rural society in the 1840s was stratified into distinct tiers. Click each level to understand who they were and how the Famine affected them differently.

Click any tier to expand details

The pyramid structure of Irish rural society meant that wealth and security decreased sharply as one descended the hierarchy. The Famine did not affect all tiers equally.

Head tenants with sufficient land often survived — some even consolidated holdings as neighbours were cleared. Under-tenants, cottiers, and labourers bore the full weight of crop failure, arrears, and forced removal.

The Fitzwilliam estate records capture this stratification in granular detail: rent ledgers distinguish holding size, emigration lists record the role of each family member, and the census columns for inhabited and uninhabited houses track the physical erasure of communities. 📚 Sources

Human Experience

What the Records Tell Us

The following are patterns drawn directly from the Coolattin emigration books and census returns — not invented stories. Each reflects what the documentary evidence shows repeatedly across the period. 📚 NLI MS 4974–4975

From the emigration books

Surrender and Demolition

A family surrendering their holding would typically see their cabin demolished within days — sometimes immediately. This pattern repeats consistently in agent correspondence. The purpose was to prevent re-occupation and to clear the land for consolidation.

From the emigration books

William Graves & Son, New Ross

The estate contracted William Graves & Son of New Ross, Co. Wexford, as the principal shipping agent. Families typically embarked at New Ross or nearby ports, bound for Quebec City. The voyage took several weeks and involved basic but recorded provisions.

From the census returns

Inhabited Houses: Before and After

The 1841 and 1851 census columns for inhabited and uninhabited houses record the visible outcome of the clearances. Some townlands show a near-complete collapse in inhabited houses between the two surveys. Explore this townland by townland in the Census Explorer.

From the emigration books

Widows and Single-Parent Families

The emigration lists include a significant proportion of widows travelling with dependent children — women whose husbands had died during the Famine years. These families are recorded by name and townland across the NLI volumes.

Pattern from records

Silence in the Archive

Many families present in the 1841 census are simply absent from 1851, with no emigration record found. Whether they died of starvation or disease, departed without estate assistance, or were evicted without passage is unknown. The gaps are themselves part of the record.

From the census returns

Those Who Stayed

Not all tenants left. Head tenants and those with larger holdings often remained, and some consolidated neighbouring land after clearances. The 1851 and 1861 census records show continuity for this tier — and the disappearance of those below them.

Aftermath

Legacy and Memory

The Coolattin clearances transformed the landscape of south Wicklow. Townlands that had supported dozens of families for generations became consolidated grazing pastures. The ridge-and-furrow pattern of former lazy beds — the physical trace of subsistence potato cultivation — can still be seen in some fields today.

In Canada and the United States, the displaced families built communities that maintained Irish language, religion, and memory across generations. Many descendants of those families are now searching for their origins — which is one reason this archive was created.

Historical memory of the clearances has always been contested. The Fitzwilliam family's defenders point to the genuine cost of the scheme and the absence of mass eviction without assistance. Critics emphasise the involuntary nature of departure, the demolition of homes, and the enrichment that followed consolidation.

This project does not adjudicate that debate. It preserves the evidence — the emigration books, the census returns, the rent rolls — so that individuals, families, and historians can read it themselves.

"The object is to get rid of those who cannot pay, and to give those who are cleared a fair start in the New World." — Agent W. Wainwright to Earl Fitzwilliam, 1848. Fitzwilliam Papers, NLI.

The Fitzwilliam Papers at the National Library of Ireland are the primary source for this research. Emigration books NLI MS 4974–4975 list the names and townlands of departing families, with notes on composition and passage arrangements.

The Coolattin Lives project at the NLI digitised and indexed these records, making them searchable for the first time. The database you are exploring was built on that work.

Sources & Further Reading

Primary Sources

  • Fitzwilliam Papers — Coolattin Estate emigration books, National Library of Ireland, MS 4974–4975. Names, townlands, family compositions, and passage arrangements, 1847–1856.
  • Fitzwilliam Papers — general correspondence, NLI, MS 6000–6099. Agent letters to the Earl; rent rolls and estate accounts.
  • Census of Ireland, 1841, 1851, 1861. Published tables — townland-level population and house statistics. National Archives of Ireland.
  • General Valuation of Ireland (Griffith's Valuation), 1847–1864. General Valuation Office, Dublin. Individual holding valuations with occupier names — allows cross-referencing with emigration lists.
  • Passenger records, Library and Archives Canada. Quebec City and Saint John arrival lists, 1847–1856.
